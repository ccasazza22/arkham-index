A data deep dive into the fiction of H. P. Lovecraft

Every name in the Mythos, counted and connected

We read Lovecraft's own stories and counted each time he names one of his gods, creatures, people, places or forbidden books, and which names turn up in the same paragraph. The web below is the result. Take the tour, or just start clicking. A small warning: reading about gods costs sanity.

      Methodology

      How the counts behind the web were made, checked and limited.

      CorpusEntriesMentionsCo-mentionsValidationLimitationsVisual encodingReproducingEvery pattern

      The Arkham Index asks one question of Lovecraft's fiction: how often does he name each god, creature, person, place and book, and which names does he put together? Every number on this site comes from one pipeline. It fetches the stories, splits them into paragraphs, runs a fixed set of search patterns over every paragraph, and records what it finds. The current corpus gives — name mentions of — entries across — stories, and — pairs of entries named in the same paragraph.

      1. Corpus

      The corpus is — stories (— words): — written by Lovecraft alone, plus — revisions and collaborations he ghost-wrote or substantially rewrote. These are tagged throughout, and the Revisions switch removes them and their counts everywhere on the site. The stories cover the Mythos-relevant fiction from "Dagon" (1917) to "The Haunter of the Dark" (1935). Juvenilia, poetry and stories with no Mythos entities are left out.

      The texts are the e-texts published at hplovecraft.com, one page per story. Paragraphs are the site's own paragraph breaks. Word counts split on whitespace. Years are dates of composition, not publication.

      2. What counts as an entry

      Entries are chosen by hand, using one canon rule: only what appears in Lovecraft's own fiction. August Derleth's additions and later writers' creations are excluded. A few names Lovecraft borrowed from friends (Tsathoggua, the Book of Eibon, von Junzt's Unaussprechlichen Kulten) are included, and flagged, because Lovecraft wrote them into his own stories.

      Each entry belongs to one of eight categories, which the web folds into four groups: gods and Great Old Ones; creatures and races; people and cults; places and books. The descriptions, epithets and "curated connections" are written by hand and are editorial. The counts are not.

      3. Counting mentions

      Each entry has one or more regular-expression patterns: its name, plus a few fixed aliases where Lovecraft uses another name for the same thing. Examples: Tulu for Cthulhu in The Mound, Outer Ones for the Mi-Go in The Whisperer in Darkness, and Black Man for Nyarlathotep in The Dreams in the Witch House. The rules:

      1. Case-sensitive, unless a pattern allows both cases ([Gg]houls?). This keeps "West" the man apart from "west" the direction.
      2. Word boundaries where needed, so \bYig\b doesn't match inside other words.
      3. Story scoping. A pattern that is ambiguous across the corpus only runs in the stories where it's unambiguous. \bWest\b runs only in Herbert West—Reanimator, \bLake\b only in At the Mountains of Madness, and Innsmouth everywhere except "Celephaïs", whose Innsmouth is a Cornish village.
      4. Normalisation. Curly apostrophes are straightened before matching, so R’lyeh and R'lyeh count the same.
      5. Every match counts. A name used three times in a paragraph counts three times.

      An entry's "Appears in" list combines the stories where it was matched with stories where a hand check found it present but unnamed, such as a narrator. Present-but-unnamed stories show a dash instead of a count.

      4. Co-mentions (the links in the web)

      Two entries are co-mentioned when both are matched in the same paragraph. Each pair counts at most once per paragraph, however many times either name appears. A link's weight is the number of paragraphs the pair shares across the corpus, and its thickness grows with that weight. The "at least" slider hides links below a chosen weight (default 2), which removes one-off coincidences.

      The paragraph is the unit because it is the smallest span where Lovecraft reliably keeps one subject in view. Sentences are too short to catch an invocation followed by its effect. Whole stories are too coarse, since everything in a story would link to everything else.

      5. Validation

      • Second source. Totals for names that can be compared directly were checked against a separate edition, the Complete Works PDF. Akeley (135), Wilbur (48), Curwen (204), Gilman (122) and the Necronomicon (49) match exactly. Other totals differ in both directions for known reasons: the PDF leaves out the five revisions (it never mentions Zamacona) and includes stories outside this corpus.
      • Review of low counts. Suspiciously small totals were read in context, which led to three corrections:
        • The Shadow over Innsmouth calls the Deep Ones "Deep Ones" only twice. Their usual names ("them things", "sea-things", "fish-frogs") were added as scoped aliases.
        • In The Shadow Out of Time, "Elder Things" means the flying polyps, not the beings of Mountains of Madness, so those matches go to the polyps.
        • "Old Ones" in Mountains of Madness excludes "Great Old Ones".
      • Readable patterns. Every entry shows its exact patterns, in its card and in the table below, so any count can be checked.

      6. Limitations

      • Pronouns aren't counted. First-person narrators come out tiny: Thurston and Dyer are named only a handful of times, and — entries are never named at all. Read a node's size as how often it's named, not how important it is.
      • Aliases are judgment calls. Adding or leaving out an alias changes a count. The choices are listed so you can disagree with them.
      • Some scoped patterns are broad. "colour" in The Colour Out of Space, "city" in Mountains of Madness and The Shadow Out of Time, and "cats" in the Ulthar stories also catch some generic uses, so those counts run high.
      • Name collisions. "Ward" also catches Charles's father, "de la Poer" catches the whole family, and both "Edward" and "Derby" are counted for Edward Derby.
      • Paragraph length varies. Charles Dexter Ward has long paragraphs, which inflates co-mentions within it.
      • One edition. Spelling and paragraph breaks follow a single e-text source.

      7. Visual encoding

      In the web, a node's radius grows with the square root of its mention count, so its area, not its width, tracks the count. Positions come from a force simulation: linked entries pull together and all entries push apart. The layout is stretched to fit the shape of the screen. Closeness means "often named together." Absolute position means nothing, and the layout changes slightly on each visit. Hollow shapes are entries that are never named. Labels are drawn in order of mention count, and a label that would overlap one already drawn is hidden until you zoom in or hover.

      Clusters. The shaded bubbles are communities found by the Louvain method, which groups entries so that more of the shared-paragraph weight falls inside groups than chance would predict. It runs on the links currently drawn, so it changes with the "at least" slider and the revisions switch. Groups with fewer than five members get no bubble. Each bubble is named after its two most-named members. The algorithm draws the groups, not an editor, so a bubble may cut across a story cycle where Lovecraft's own crossovers pull it.

      The four group colours were checked with a colour-vision validator against the page background, for every pair of colours. All four pass the standard tests for colour-blind and full-colour readers. Each group also has its own shape, so colour is never the only cue. The heatmap uses a single-hue scale on a square-root curve, so rare names stay visible next to common ones.

      8. Reproducing the numbers

      The pipeline is in the project repository under pipeline/:

      1. python pipeline/fetch_texts.py downloads the stories.
      2. python pipeline/extract.py splits them into paragraphs.
      3. python pipeline/count.py applies the patterns and writes data/counts.json.
      4. python pipeline/build.py builds the site into public/.

      The patterns live in pipeline/count.py. Change one, re-run the last two steps, and every chart updates.
